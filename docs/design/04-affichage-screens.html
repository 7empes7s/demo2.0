Civic Companion · community screens · 6 October 2026

The restof the app

Nineteen screens in Affichage: the ideas board, an idea with residents' arguments, the composer and what happens when someone crosses the line, a resident's own page, a sounding, a real ballot with delegation, a debate room and its setup, and the letter that says you were drawn for a panel, then pictures, documents, follow-through on what was voted, flagging a result, and the mandate scorecard, then first run and sign-in, results, the panel room, a delegate's page and the companion conversation. Scroll inside the phones. Tap the buttons.

Rules these screens obey

  • Participation is private. No names, photos or pseudonyms are ever shown. People appear as what they are to the file: a resident of Esch, a certified reviewer, a panel member.
  • Nothing here is a vote except the ballot. Supporting an idea, taking a stance, rating an argument: none of it counts as a vote, and the words say so.
  • Reach, not importance. Ideas carry a reach (national, regional, local, small) computed from how many people they affect. Never a priority label.
  • Vocabulary file applies. Resident, file, idea, support, argue the other side, panel drawn by lot, secure sign-in.

Button lab

Six ways to press

The amber sheet behind the button was the odd one. Five alternatives below, each with the same press model: hold flattens, release springs back and flips the label. Tap any sample. Marouane picked ink backing; it is the default below and on every button on this page. The others stay for the record.

Amber backing

The current one. A second amber sheet turned a degree. Loud, and the backing competes with the amber face once chosen.

Ink backing

Same model, black sheet, straight, four pixels. Quieter, matches the stapled sheets. Chosen.

Flat

No backing at all. Press sinks it a little; release pops it and fills amber. The calmest.

Stapled

Two staples hold it to the wall and bend while you press. Playful, very much ours.

Highlighter

The face stays paper; on release an amber marker sweeps under the words. Like someone marked the notice.

Ink block

Solid ink with paper text; chosen flips it to amber with ink text. The strongest contrast, good for the one main action per screen.

01 · Agora

Ideas from residents

A board of pinned ideas, sorted by reach first and support second. New ideas hide their support count for a day so nothing snowballs early.

Each idea is a sheet. The reach tag is computed by Scope from affected population, so it is shown as a fact, not a badge the proposer chose. Support is one tap per human, held by secure sign-in; until that is live the button says so instead of pretending. Proposers are never named: when an idea reaches national attention you see the proposer's track record, not who they are.

Components

  • Reach filter chips (All, National, Regional, Local, Small)
  • Idea sheet: reach, where, support count or "new, count tomorrow"
  • Support button with press and tack
  • Track-record line for ideas that reached national attention

Motion

  • Sheets paste in, staggered 80 ms
  • Support: press, tack lands, count flaps up by one
  • Filter change: peel the old list

02 · Commons

An idea and its arguments

Comments are arguments, and they rank by bridging: an argument rises when residents who disagree with each other both rate it strong. The loudest side cannot win by volume.

Each argument shows its stance, who it comes from as a role (a resident of the area, a certified reviewer), and the grade of any factual claim inside it, checked against official documents. Ratings are "strong" or "weak", never likes. The composer sits at the bottom, with the rules one tap away.

Components

  • Stance tabs: For · Against · Both
  • Argument sheet: stance tag, role, text with graded claims, strong/weak
  • "Rated strong by both sides" line
  • Composer field with stance picker

Motion

  • Rating: press; the sheet lifts one notch when it rises
  • New argument: pasted at the top with a tack

03 · Commons

The composer

Writing an argument is a small form, not a chat box: pick a stance, write, attach a source. Before it posts, the Companion reads it back and tells you how it will be graded.

Two helpers run before posting. The claim check grades factual sentences against official documents and shows the grade inline, so a resident can fix a wrong number before the crowd sees it. The civility check looks for insults and threats; it never judges opinions. Both run on the device where possible, and the resident always sees exactly which words triggered a notice.

States

  • Writing, with a live character count
  • Checking: marching ants
  • Checked: graded claims shown inline, post enabled
  • Blocked: see the next screen

Motion

  • Checking: march
  • A grade appears with a press
  • Post: the sheet peels up to the list

04 · Moderation and errors

When something can't be posted

A red sheet with a tack. It names the words, says the rule in one line, and offers the fix. It never says "inappropriate content" and never hides what was flagged.

The civility rule is narrow on purpose: no insults at a person, no threats, no slurs. Opinions, anger at a policy and strong language about a file are allowed. The first notice is just a block with an edit. A second within a month pauses posting for a day. Repeated cases go to a panel of residents drawn by lot, never to a moderator's taste. The lengths are Charter values and can change by vote. Below the civility block are the other errors the app needs, in the same voice.

Error sheets

  • Civility block, with the flagged words highlighted
  • Posting paused (second notice)
  • Referred to a panel (repeated)
  • Secure sign-in not ready
  • Offline, saved copy shown
  • Claim too long

Motion

  • Block: the composer shakes once, the red sheet pastes on top
  • Edit: the red sheet tears away

06 · Poll

A sounding

A poll is called a sounding, and the sheet says in capitals that it is not a vote. It helps a panel pick which options to draft; it decides nothing.

Results are bars in ink and amber, with the hatched bar for people who said "not sure". Each option links to its strongest argument from the Commons. The count of participants is shown as a flap number so it feels like a tally board, not a chart.

Components

  • "Not a vote" banner

  • Who asked, and what happens next

07 · Booth

A ballot

The one place the word vote is used. Secret, re-votable until it closes, with no receipt by design. Or hand it to your delegate on this topic and spend your weekly budget elsewhere.

The budget line ("3 of 5 this week") comes from Pulse and the Charter. The delegate card is a public person with a name, because power is public while participation is private. After casting, the confirmation sheet explains why there is no receipt: so nobody can make you prove how you voted. A verification code lets you check that your ballot is in the tally without revealing it.

Components

08 · Debates

A debate room

A structured room with four rounds, a visible clock, one speaker at a time, and a panel of residents drawn by lot holding the gavel. The crowd rates arguments, asks questions and sees claims graded live.

Rooms are not open chat. They run on a schedule, with a side speaking for a fixed time and the other replying. The Companion grades factual claims as they are made and the grade appears under the argument. The second phone is the setup sheet: who may speak, the rounds, the clock, the language, whether claims are checked live, whether it is recorded. All of it is pinned to the room so every participant sees the same rules.

Room

  • Round track: Opening · Evidence · Rebuttal · Closing
  • Clock and who holds the floor
  • Current argument sheet with live grade
  • Strong / Weak / Ask a question
  • Questions queue, picked by the panel

Setup options

  • Who may speak: affected residents, certified reviewers, anyone
  • Rounds and minutes per turn
  • Language, with live translation on or off
  • Live claim check on or off
  • Recorded to the public record or not

The letter that makes the system real: five residents drawn by lot to review a plan, and you are one of them. It lands in your inbox with the proof of the draw and a plain yes or no.

10 · Pictures on an idea

Prints, taped on

A photo is a print taped to the sheet, not a hero image. Four at most, one tapped as the cover. The idea stays the headline; the pictures are its proof.

Pictures are the fastest way a resident shows a broken bench or a blocked crossing, and the fastest way to leak where they live. So the phone strips the location, the time and the device from the file before it leaves, blurs faces and plates, and the resident types the place. Each print carries a small line: what the app removed and when it was taken, by the resident's own word. A print with a red tag was supplied by a resident; a navy tag is an official photo from the file. No filters, no cropping tools: one tap takes it, one tap makes it the cover.

Components

  • Print: white border, tape, caption with place and "by a resident"
  • Cover: straightened and lifted, red tack
  • Add print: dashed slot, counts 3 of 4
  • Privacy line under the prints
  • Full-size view: print fills the screen on ink, pinch to zoom

Motion

  • A new print pastes with the tape landing last
  • Cover: the tapped print straightens, the old cover tilts back
  • Remove: peel

11 · Documents and attachments

Papers with a source

Every document says where it came from. Official papers wear a navy corner and the council's own address; a resident's receipt or letter wears red and says "supplied by a resident, checked by nobody yet".

A file carries the council's documents, and arguments can carry a resident's evidence: a receipt, a letter from the commune, a measurement. The two must never look alike. Provenance records the original's address and hash for official papers; resident attachments show the same chain once a resident drawn by lot has looked at them. Until then they are dashed, readable, and not counted as evidence. Attachments are the same three kinds everywhere: a picture, a document, a link. A link is shown as a card with its address spelled out, never as a bare blue word.

Components

  • Document row: page icon, title, source line, page count, date
  • Source tags: Official (navy), Resident (red), Checked (stamp)
  • Pending: dashed row, "waiting for a check"
  • Add attachment: three slots, picture, document, link
  • Link card: title and the full address

Motion

  • Documents paste in a stack, last on top
  • A check completes: the dashed border fills and the stamp slams

12 · Follow-through

Did they do it?

Every plan that residents voted gets a sheet that outlives the vote: what was promised, by when, and what residents on the ground say. Three flags, and a panel drawn by lot settles it.

This is the loop closing. The council reports "done"; residents who walk past it every day say otherwise with a flag and a print. The flags are Not done, Needs work and Done badly. Each resident flags once per plan and can change it. When flags pass the Charter threshold (placeholder: 100, or 5% of the voters on that plan), a panel of residents drawn by lot inspects within 14 days and stamps the sheet. Their verdict feeds the scorecard at the end of the mandate. The council's own claim is shown next to the residents' count, never instead of it. A missed date turns red and stays red.

Components

  • Plan sheet: promise, due date, who answers for it
  • Progress bar: council's claim (hatched) vs panel-verified (ink)
  • Flag counts with bars, threshold marker
  • Panel stamp: Not done / Needs work / Done
  • Prints from residents in a strip

Motion

  • Overdue: the date flips to red with a flap
  • A new flag: the bar grows, the count flaps
  • Panel verdict: stamp slams, sheet gets a red tack

13 · Flagging a result

Say what is wrong, show it

Three press buttons, one print, one line. A flag is a report, not a complaint: it asks for the place and the picture, and it tells you what your flag will trigger.

The flag composer is deliberately short so people flag from the pavement. Choosing a flag is the same press model as a ballot option. A print is optional for "not done" and expected for "done badly", because that claim needs eyes. The sheet shows the running count and the threshold so the resident sees what one more flag does. The errors are the same family as elsewhere: a second flag on the same plan becomes a change, not a duplicate; a flag on a plan whose panel just said "done" asks for a print first.

Components

  • Flag picker: three press buttons, exclusive
  • Print slot, place field
  • Count line: "joins 213 others · panel at 100"
  • Error: already flagged, change instead
  • Error: panel said done, print required
  • Confirmation: small red tack on the plan sheet

Motion

  • Pick: press, the other two dim
  • Send: the sheet peels off and a red tack lands on the plan

14 · Mandate scorecard

The report card

At the end of a mandate the follow-through sheets add up to one card per office: what was voted, what a panel verified, and the residents' rating. Under half, and the office holder cannot stand again.

This is the part of the vision that gives the vote teeth, so it must be the plainest sheet in the app. No narrative, no spin: each plan is one row with the panel's verdict, then one rating by residents, Kept their word or Did not, with the same secret ballot as a vote. The threshold is a Charter value. The card is public, carries the office and the name, because power is public, and it is the one place a name is printed large. The amber card is a passing one; the red card is the bar.

Components

  • Office header: name, office, mandate dates
  • Rows: plan, panel verdict stamp
  • Totals with flap digits
  • Rating sheet: two press buttons, secret
  • Verdict card: amber passes, red bars

Motion

  • Rows paste one by one, verdict stamps last
  • Totals flap up
  • The verdict card pastes with the longer settle and a tack

15 · First run and secure sign-in

Five sheets, then the wall

Language, place, topics, and only then the question of signing in. Everything before sign-in works without it. The credential sheet is navy because it is official, and it says in one line what it proves and what it never tells anyone.

Most people will open the app from a neighbour's link or a poster. They must get to their week in under a minute with no account. Sign-in comes later, the first time they want to support, post or vote, and it happens once: the eID or EUDI wallet issues a credential that says "resident of Esch, adult, valid until", nothing more, and the issuer forgets the session. The sheet shows the three facts the credential carries and the five it does not. Languages are the five people here actually speak; the first sheet is in all five at once so nobody has to find English to pick Portuguese.

Sheets

  • Language, five words in five languages
  • Your place: commune, then street if you want more reach
  • Topics you follow
  • Your first week, no sign-in needed
  • Secure sign-in, when first needed: what it proves, what it never shares

Motion

  • Each sheet pastes over the last; the stack stays visible at the edge
  • Credential issued: the navy card pastes with the long settle and a stamp "valid"

16 · Results and verification

Counted, and you can check

The result is a tally sheet with big numbers, turnout, and a line per option. Under it, the proof: your code, the three checks anyone can run, and the address of the public log. Secret ballots, public count.

Booth ballots are receipt-free, so the app can never show "you voted A". What it can show is that a ballot with your code is in the tally, that the tally matches the public log, and that the log has not changed since it was sealed. Three checks, each a line with a square that fills. The result page is the one that gets screenshotted and argued over, so it has no decoration: ink numbers, red for the winner's margin, the drand round and the log hash in mono for those who want them. Delegated ballots are shown as their own number, never folded in silently.

Components

  • Tally rows: option, bar, percentage
  • Turnout and delegated share with flap digits
  • Verification sheet: your code, three checks
  • Public log line: round, hash, address
  • What happens next: who executes, due date, follow-through link

Motion

  • Bars grow from zero when the sheet pastes
  • Checks fill one by one with the press settle
  • Winner's margin flaps in red

17 · Panel work room

Seven people, one table

After the letter comes the room: the reading pack, the agenda with dates, the draft options the panel writes together, and the day they hand it to the residents. The panel's pay is a line, not a secret.

A panel drawn by lot has fourteen days to turn a file into two or three options residents can vote on. The room gives them the official documents with the same source tags, the experts' answers as pinned sheets, and one shared draft where each member's edit is marked. Members are letters, not names, inside the room; outside it, only the panel's output is published. The agenda is a wall calendar with the days crossed off. The room has one rule in red at the top: the panel writes options, it does not pick one.

Components

  • Rule line in red
  • Members as letters, you in ink
  • Agenda with crossed-off days
  • Reading pack: documents with source tags, "read by 5 of 7"
  • Shared draft with marked edits
  • Expert answers as pinned sheets
  • Pay and expenses line (Charter value)
  • Hand-over button, needs 5 of 7

Motion

  • Days cross off with a tear
  • An edit by another member pastes in amber then settles to paper

18 · A delegate's public page

Power is public

The only page in the app with a person's name printed large. A delegate is a public person: what they said they would do, how they cast on every round, how many residents trust them per topic, and the one button that takes it back.

Participation is private and power is public, so a delegate gives up the secrecy a resident keeps: their ballots are open by choice, and the page is built from that record, not from their own description. The record is a list of rounds, each with how they cast and whether it matched what they announced. Residents who delegate see a line: "your ballots this year cast by this person: 4". The topic scope is explicit, so a mobility delegate shows nothing on housing. The take-back button is red and immediate. There are no followers, no messages, no photo: the avatar is the § like everyone's, the name is the only difference.

Components

  • Name, topics, "public since"
  • Trust count per topic with flap digits
  • Record: rounds, how cast, announced vs cast
  • Their standing statements, dated
  • Your line: ballots cast for you this year
  • Take back / delegate buttons per topic

Motion

  • Record rows paste in a stack
  • Take back: the delegation sheet tears off
  • A mismatch between announced and cast flaps to red

19 · The companion conversation

Ask the file anything

A conversation pinned to one file. Every answer cites a page of the official document, says plainly when it is guessing, and never tells you how to vote. Three reading levels and a read-aloud, because the people who need this most are not the people who read PDFs.

The companion is the fastest piece to ship and the easiest to get wrong. The design keeps it honest by form: answers are paper sheets with a § corner, each claim carries a small page tag that opens the source, and the words "I am not sure" are a red line, not a footnote. The resident's own words are amber sheets. The level switch (Simple, Normal, Full) is on the answer, so one tap rewrites it. Asking "what should I vote" gets the same refusal every time, with the two sides and the arena instead. Voice in, voice out, in the five languages.

Components

  • Resident sheet (amber), companion sheet (paper, § corner)
  • Page tags that open the source
  • Level switch on each answer
  • "Not sure" red line
  • Refusal sheet: the two sides, the arena
  • Ask bar with microphone; read-aloud per sheet

Motion

  • Answers paste line by line, page tags last
  • Level change: the sheet flips (the label flip, bigger)
  • Read-aloud: a march line runs under the sheet
